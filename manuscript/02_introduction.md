# Introduction

Osteomyelitis is a destructive bone infection that requires long antibiotic courses and often surgery¹. Its spine-specific forms, vertebral osteomyelitis (VOM, ICD-10 M46.2) and discitis/spondylodiscitis (M46.4), are common in adults, and the case numbers keep climbing as populations age and spinal instrumentation spreads². Imaging, microbiology and source-control surgery have all improved, but the host question — *who* develops vertebral or disc-space infection — is still poorly answered³.

There is no shortage of candidate answers on the immune side. Neutrophils and monocytes/macrophages clear bacteria early; lymphocytes and eosinophils shape granuloma formation and chronicity; IL-1β, IL-6 (acting through IL-6R), the chemokines CXCL10/IP-10 and CCL2/MCP-1, the acute-phase reactant CRP, and CD40 signalling on antigen-presenting cells all sit in the pathway⁴⁻⁶. The long-standing hypothesis is therefore that *trait-level* variation in these effectors causally shapes osteomyelitis susceptibility⁷, but — and this is the part worth being precise about — no MR or randomised study has actually tested it.

**What is wrong with the observational evidence.** Routine clinical data link immune markers to osteomyelitis all the time, but the link is confounded in three overlapping ways. CRP and leukocytosis in an established infection are a *consequence* of the infection, not a cause of susceptibility (reverse causation). And the obvious shared drivers (age, diabetes, renal impairment, smoking, recent bacteraemia) distort any naïve association⁸⁻¹⁰. Trials that would settle this by giving people immune-modifying drugs and watching whether they get fewer bone infections are not ethical and probably not feasible at scale. So we genuinely do not know whether, or in which direction, these traits causally affect osteomyelitis risk.

**Why Mendelian randomization, and why now.** Mendelian randomization (MR) gets around the confounding problem by using germline variants as instruments for an exposure: alleles are randomly allocated at conception and cannot be modified by disease status¹¹,¹². The STROBE-MR framework gives explicit reporting rules for these designs¹³. A 2025 two-sample MR study using UK Biobank and FinnGen data reported no causal association between infective endocarditis and osteomyelitis¹⁴, but — to our knowledge — no MR to date has interrogated the *immune* determinants of osteomyelitis, and none has exploited the **spine-specific** phenotypes that have just become available in FinnGen R11 (VOM M46.2, discitis M46.4, pyogenic intervertebral-disc infection, other infective spondylopathies)¹⁵. Earlier osteomyelitis MR work has had to lump all sites together, which obviously masks spine-specific biology if it exists.

**What we set out to do, and what we expect to find.** We estimated the causal effect of 11 genetically predicted immune/cytokine traits on five osteomyelitis phenotypes, four of them spine-specific. Our prior, pre-specified in the analysis plan, was that life-course variation in circulating immune effectors is unlikely to be a strong directional driver of osteomyelitis risk, including the spine-specific subtypes. Secondary aims were to map directionality, heterogeneity and power, and to test whether spine-specific subtypes behave differently from all-site osteomyelitis. We report a rigorously null result with the full sensitivity stack attached — partly because we expected one, and partly because the recent MR literature has been polluted by small-outcome-driven positive findings that do not survive robustness checks¹⁶, and a well-conducted null is worth showing in detail.
